Choosing your directionاختيار مسارك

Offensive or defensive is an evidence question, not a personality one. Here is how to answer it — and what months 4–6 look like once you have.الهجومي أم الدفاعي؟ سؤال يُجاب عليه بالأدلة، لا بالمزاج. هذه هي الطريقة — وكيف تبدو الأشهر ٤–٦ بعد أن تقرر.

The detailed content on this page is deliberately in English: the terminology of both tracks is what you will be asked about in interviews.المحتوى التفصيلي لهذه الصفحة بالإنجليزية عمداً: مصطلحات المسارين هي نفسها التي ستُسأل عنها في المقابلات.

Read this in Week 11, not Week 13. You need to be paying attention during the taster weeks, not trying to remember them afterward.

First, the honest framing

Thirteen weeks does not make anyone an expert. It makes someone who has a genuine, tested foundation — and who can now learn any specialization without drowning. That's the real achievement, and it's a big one: most people who "study cybersecurity" never get here.

You are not yet hireable in a specialist role. Not because the program failed, but because a foundation isn't a specialty. Months 4-6 build the specialty. Anyone who starts applying for pentest jobs in Week 14 and hears nothing back will wrongly conclude the 13 weeks didn't work.

And the choice you make now is not permanent. Most senior people in this field are fluent in both offense and defense — they started somewhere and the paths converged. This is a starting direction, not a life sentence.

How to choose: evidence, not vibes

The most common career mistake in security is choosing a specialty in month one based on what sounds cool, then discovering in year two that you hate the daily work.

So do it with evidence. Answer these for each of weeks 11 and 12:

Question Offensive (W12) Defensive (W11)
Which part did I lose track of time doing?
Which part did I have to force myself through?
What did I do beyond the assignment, unprompted?
When I got stuck, did I want to keep digging or move on?
Which felt more like "work" and which more like "play"?
Did I enjoy the report/write-up or resent it?

Two signals matter more than the rest:

  1. Where did you go beyond the assignment? That's intrinsic motivation, and it's the only thing that sustains you through the boring parts of a real job.
  2. How do you feel about the paperwork? Offensive work is ~60% reporting and documentation. Defensive work is heavy on process, tickets, and communication. People who love the technical part but hate writing will burn out in both. Be honest here.

Also weigh the environment, not just the activity:

Offensive Defensive
Daily rhythm Project-based, variable, deadline spikes Shift-based, steady, on-call rotations
Team shape Small teams, solo work common Larger SOC teams, heavy collaboration
Stress profile High-stakes deadlines; "did I miss something" Sustained alert volume; "did I miss the one that mattered"
Visibility Deliverable is a report; clear endpoint Deliverable is absence of incidents — harder to demonstrate
Learning curve Very steep, very wide Steep, narrower, deeper
Who thrives Curious, self-directed, comfortable with ambiguity Methodical, patient, process-comfortable, communicative

Neither column is better. They suit genuinely different temperaments, and plenty of people discover — often in their 30s — that they were on the wrong side.

The third option nobody mentions

You don't have to pick either.

Path What it is Why it's underrated
GRC (Governance, Risk, Compliance) Policy, risk assessment, audit, frameworks Less technical, high demand, excellent entry point, strong career ceiling
AppSec Security embedded in a dev team Hybrid — you need both offense and defense instincts
Cloud Security Securing AWS/Azure/GCP The fastest-growing area, huge shortage
Detection Engineering Building the detections, not just triaging them Sits between blue team and engineering; very in demand
Purple Team Explicitly bridging both Increasingly a real role, not just an exercise
Security Engineering Building security into infrastructure Deep systems work, less adversarial

If after the taster weeks neither offense nor defense felt right, that is real information, not a failure. Say so, and we design a different Month 4.

What Months 4-6 look like

Not week-by-week — that depends on who's still standing and what they choose. But the shape:

If Offensive

Month Focus
4 Depth on web exploitation (PortSwigger Academy, systematically) + privilege escalation (Linux, then Windows) + Active Directory fundamentals — AD is the single biggest gap between beginners and employability
5 Network penetration testing, pivoting, Metasploit, exploitation methodology against full machines (HTB/VulnHub), reporting at professional standard
6 Specialize: exploit development, reverse engineering, cloud pentesting, or red teaming. Build a portfolio of write-ups. Certification prep (OSCP or PNPT)

The gap to close: Active Directory. Nearly every real corporate environment runs it, and almost every beginner curriculum skips it. It is not optional.

If Defensive

Month Focus
4 Deepen detection: SIEM engineering (Wazuh/Elastic), log source onboarding, detection rule writing (Sigma), tuning and false-positive reduction. This is the most employable blue-team skill
5 Incident response in depth, digital forensics (disk, memory, network), malware analysis basics, threat intelligence and how to consume it
6 Specialize: SOC engineering, threat hunting, DFIR, cloud security, or detection engineering. Build a home-lab portfolio with real detection scenarios. Certification prep (BTL1, GCIH, or Security+ first)

The gap to close: real detection volume. Free labs give you clean, well-labelled data. Real environments are noisy. Build a home lab that generates realistic noise, and learn to tune against it.

Either way, for everyone

  • Python properly. You've had Bash. Python is the language of security tooling and you need it in month 4 regardless of track.
  • Build a portfolio. A public repo of write-ups from HTB/TryHackMe/VulnHub. This gets people hired more reliably than certifications.
  • A personal blog. Explaining your work in writing is the skill that compounds fastest in this career.

The employability conversation — have it honestly

What gets someone hired at month 6:

  1. Demonstrable hands-on skill (a portfolio of things they actually did)
  2. Ability to communicate (write-ups, reports, explaining findings)
  3. A foundational cert (Security+, or a track-specific one)
  4. Evidence they can learn independently

What does not:

  • A pile of certificates with no portfolio
  • Being able to name tools without having used them deeply
  • Enthusiasm without evidence

Job market reality, regionally: SOC analyst and GRC roles are the most accessible entry points and the most numerous. Pentest roles are more competitive and usually expect prior IT or SOC experience. If someone's goal is "get into the field," the SOC is usually the faster door — and it's a legitimate destination, not a consolation prize.

The honest sentence for Week 13:

"You've built a real foundation and you can now learn anything in this field. You are not yet a specialist, and you shouldn't expect to be hired as one yet. Months 4-6 make you hireable. The hardest part of this is behind you — not because the material gets easier, but because you now know how to learn it."

The decision meeting — Week 13

Run it as a structured session, not a chat:

  1. Each learner presents their taster-week evidence table. Read from the notes, not from memory.
  2. They state a preference and one sentence of justification.
  3. You give a candid assessment: where their demonstrated strengths actually point — which may not match their preference.
  4. If preference and aptitude disagree, say so. A learner who loved offense but showed weak debugging and no patience for reporting needs to hear that now, not in year two.
  5. Record the decision in the progress tracker with the reasoning. Revisit at the end of Month 4.
  6. Nobody is locked in. Make that explicit.

What we don't know yet — and how we decide

Months 4-6 are deliberately not designed yet. Designing them now, before knowing:

  • who's still in the program
  • what they actually chose
  • what turned out to be the cohort's weak spots
  • how much time they realistically still have

...would waste the work and produce a plan built on guesses.

Design them at the end of Week 13, from what the cohort actually looks like.
